Style and narrative voice can be viewed as two separate ideas,
with style belonging more to the author as the creator,
and narrative voice belonging more to the individual story or character.
Narrative voice relates to who is telling the story, whereas style is how the story is told.
The author has the power to change both, from story to story.
Voice is related to identity and the character's emotional experience.
Look at two coming-of-age novels told in first-person point of view with different voices.
The Catcher in the Rye has Holden's sardonic, sling-filled voice.
The Perks of Being a Wallflower has Charlie's earnest and reflective letters.

Narrative voice is the reader's entry point into a story, and a way to establish immediate
emotional engagement. Many literary agents and editors are looking for novels that are
voicey, meaning books where the writing pops off the page with personality. Not just anybody could
tell this story. It had to be this author, and this character, all with the intent of
inspiring feeling in the reader, whether it's curiosity or disgust, fear or wonder.
Keep in mind that it's not just word choice and sentence structure that contributes to voice,
as author Joss Sippe cautions on Gotham Writers. Sippe references a definition of voice from Meg
Rossoff as the deepest possible reflection of who you are, and that the job of your voice
is not to seduce or flatter or make well-shaped sentences,
